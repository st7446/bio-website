Protein Synthesis

The process by which cells build proteins according to genetic instructions. This central dogma (DNA → RNA → Protein) is fundamental to all life.

The Central Dogma

DNA carries genetic information encoded in nucl nucleotide sequences. This information flows: DNA → RNA → Protein. Each step involves specific enzymes and machinery.

DNA → DNA (Replication)

DNA polymerase replicates the entire genome before cell division. Semi-conservative replication creates two identical DNA molecules from one. Essential for heredity and genetic continuity across generations.

DNA → RNA (Transcription)

RNA polymerase reads DNA template and synthesizes complementary RNA (mRNA, tRNA, rRNA). Only specific genes are transcribed. Regulatory proteins control which genes are expressed. Post-transcriptional processing adds 5' cap and 3' poly-A tail.

RNA → Protein (Translation)

Ribosomes read mRNA sequences and assemble proteins. tRNAs bring amino acids matching mRNA codons. Peptide bonds form between amino acids. Process involving ~100 different ribosomal proteins and rRNAs.

RNA → RNA (Reverse Transcription)

Reverse transcriptase (from retroviruses) synthesizes DNA from RNA template. Used naturally by retroviruses (HIV). Used experimentally for studying gene expression. Not typically part of central dogma in normal cells.

Transcription: DNA to mRNA

Initiation

RNA polymerase binds to promoter region. Transcription factor proteins help recruit polymerase. Promoter sequences (TATA box) signal polymerase where to start. Different genes have different promoter strengths.

Elongation

RNA polymerase moves along DNA template strand, synthesizing mRNA. Uses nucleotriphosphates (ATP, GTP, CTP, UTP). Rate: ~50 nucleotides/second in bacteria, ~20/second in eukaryotes. Proofreading maintains accuracy.

Termination

Transcription ends at termination signals (stop codons or sequences). In prokaryotes, Rho protein may assist. In eukaryotes, cleavage and polyadenylation signals direct termination. mRNA is released as primary transcript.

Post-Transcriptional Processing (Eukaryotes)

5' capping: 7-methylguanosine cap added. 3' polyadenylation: ~200 adenines added. Splicing: introns removed, exons ligated by snRNPs. Alternative splicing creates multiple proteins from single gene.

mRNA Export

Processed mRNA exits nucleus through nuclear pore complexes. In cytoplasm, mRNA binds ribosomes for translation. mRNA can be transported to specific cell locations. Localized translation enables spatial organization of proteins.

mRNA Stability

5' cap and poly-A tail protect from degradation. Regulatory proteins (RNA-binding proteins) stabilize or destabilize mRNA. Average mRNA half-life: 5 hours in bacteria, 1-10 hours in eukaryotes.

Translation: mRNA to Protein

🏭 Ribosome Structure

Two subunits: 60S (eukaryotes) or 50S (bacteria), and 40S/30S. Contains rRNA and ribosomal proteins. Three tRNA binding sites: A (aminoacyl), P (peptidyl), E (exit). Catalyzes peptide bond formation.

Initiation

mRNA binds ribosome at ribosome binding site (Shine-Dalgarno in bacteria). Initiator tRNA brings formyl-methionine (fMet) or methionine in eukaryotes. Initiation factors (IF2, others) aid assembly. AUG start codon recognized.

Elongation Cycle

Three steps: 1) EF-Tu brings aminoacyl-tRNA to A site. 2) Peptidyl transferase catalyzes bond between P-site and A-site amino acids. 3) Translocase moves tRNAs: P→E, A→P, emptying A site for next tRNA. Each cycle makes one bond.

Proofreading & Accuracy

Error rate: ~1 mistake per 10,000 codons. tRNA cognate recognition by ribosome. EF-Tu GTPase adds second proofreading step. Codon-anticodon pairing verified twice. Ensures genetic code is accurately read.

Termination

Stop codons (UAA, UAG, UGA) have no corresponding tRNA. Release factors (RF1/RF2 in bacteria, eRF1 in eukaryotes) bind release factors recognize stop codons. Hydrolysis of final peptide-tRNA bond releases polypeptide.

Speed & Efficiency

Rate: ~20 amino acids/second in eukaryotes, ~20 in bacteria. Polyribosomes (multiple ribosomes on single mRNA). Single mRNA translated many times simultaneously. Synthesis takes ~1 minute for average protein (400 amino acids).

Post-Translational Modifications

Signal Sequences & Targeting

Signal peptides direct proteins to specific destinations: ER (membrane proteins, secreted), mitochondria, chloroplasts, nucleus. Signal Recognition Particle (SRP) recognizes ER signal and directs ribosome. Targeting often studied by fluorescent protein tags.

Protein Folding

Nascent polypeptide begins folding co-translationally. Chaperone proteins (Hsp70, Hsp90) assist folding and prevent aggregation. GroEL/ES forms cage for folding. Misfolded proteins cleared by proteasome via ubiquitination.

Phosphorylation

Addition of phosphate groups to serine, threonine, tyrosine residues. Catalyzed by kinases, removed by phosphatases. Major regulatory mechanism for cell signaling and protein activity. ~30% of proteins are phosphorylated.

Glycosylation

Attachment of carbohydrate groups (N-linked on asparagine, O-linked on serine/threonine). Affects protein folding, stability, recognition. Glycoproteins important for cell signaling, immune recognition. ~50% of blood plasma proteins are glycoproteins.

Ubiquitination & Degradation

Ubiquitin (76 aa protein) covalently attached to lysines. Poly-ubiquitin chains mark proteins for proteasome degradation. Enables precise control of protein levels. Dysregulation in cancer and neurodegeneration.

Proteolytic Cleavage

Removal of signal peptides and pro-sequences by proteases. Activates zymogens (inactive enzyme precursors). Examples: insulin (from preproinsulin), caspases (apoptosis). Irreversible modification controlling protein activity.
