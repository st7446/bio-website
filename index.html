// Section 02

Introduction to Proteins

Proteins are amino-acid polymers that fold into precise 3D structures to perform catalytic, structural, transport, signaling, and immune functions. Their activity depends on sequence, folding, environment, and interaction partners.

// Section 03

Twenty Amino Acids

The 20 standard amino acids differ in side-chain charge, polarity, and size. These properties determine hydrophobic cores, surface interactions, catalytic chemistry, and how proteins fold and function.

// Section 04

Four Structural Levels

Protein architecture is hierarchical: primary sequence, secondary motifs, tertiary fold, and quaternary assembly. Stability comes from hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic packing, ionic interactions, and disulfide bridges.

// Section 05

3D Molecule Viewer

Interactive 3D models let you inspect helices, sheets, active sites, ligand pockets, and subunit interfaces. Comparing folds across proteins makes structure-function relationships far clearer than static diagrams.

// Section 06

Secondary Structure Deep Dive

Secondary structure arises from local backbone hydrogen bonding. Alpha-helices, beta-sheets, and connecting turns are constrained by phi/psi angles and side-chain sterics, which shape stability and flexibility.

// Section 07

Protein Families

Proteins are grouped into families by shared sequence motifs, conserved domains, and fold patterns. Family-level analysis helps predict function for unknown proteins and explains evolutionary relationships.

// Section 08

Experimental Techniques

Modern structural biology combines X-ray crystallography, cryo-EM, NMR spectroscopy, mass spectrometry, and computational prediction. Each method balances resolution, sample requirements, throughput, and cost.

// Section 09

Protein Profiles

Profile studies connect molecular form to real biology. Hemoglobin demonstrates cooperative oxygen binding, insulin illustrates endocrine signaling, and collagen explains tensile strength in connective tissues.

// Section 10

Protein Data Bank

The Protein Data Bank (PDB) is the central global archive of experimentally determined macromolecular structures. Researchers use PDB files for visualization, docking, mutation analysis, and hypothesis generation.

// Section 11

Diseases Encyclopedia

Many disorders arise from altered protein structure, folding, or aggregation. Point mutations can disrupt function, while misfolded proteins can accumulate and damage tissues, especially in neurodegenerative conditions.

// Section 12

Protein Synthesis

Protein synthesis follows DNA transcription and mRNA translation. Ribosomes decode codons, tRNAs deliver amino acids, and the growing chain exits the ribosome for folding, targeting, and post-translational modification.

// Section 13

Protein Evolution & Bioinformatics

Protein evolution is traced through sequence conservation, domain shuffling, duplication, and divergence. Bioinformatics tools reveal conserved residues, predict function, and map phylogenetic relationships.

// Section 14

Interactive Quiz

The quiz section reinforces key ideas from amino-acid chemistry, folding forces, structural hierarchy, databases, and disease mechanisms. Use it to self-assess and target weak concepts before exams.

// Section 15

Searchable Glossary

Use the glossary as a fast reference for core molecular biology and biochemistry vocabulary, from primary structure and allostery to chaperones, quaternary assemblies, and conformational change.
