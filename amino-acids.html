The 20 Amino Acids

Amino acids are the building blocks of proteins. Each of the 20 standard amino acids has a unique side chain (R group) that determines its chemical properties and affects how proteins fold and function.

Amino Acid Properties

🟦 Hydrophobic (Nonpolar)

These amino acids have nonpolar side chains that repel water. They cluster together in the interior of proteins, creating a hydrophobic core. Examples: Alanine, Valine, Leucine, Isoleucine, Methionine, Phenylalanine, Tryptophan, Proline.

💠 Polar Uncharged

Polar amino acids have side chains that can form hydrogen bonds with water or other molecules. They're often found on protein surfaces. Examples: Serine, Threonine, Asparagine, Glutamine, Cysteine, Tyrosine.

🔴🔵 Charged (Ionic)

Charged amino acids have ionizable side chains that can gain or lose protons. Acidic residues (Asp, Glu) are negatively charged; Basic residues (Lys, Arg, His) are positively charged. They're critical for binding and catalysis.

⚪ Special Cases

Glycine lacks a side chain (just H), making it extremely flexible. Proline's ring structure restricts rotation. Cysteine can form disulfide bonds (disulfide bridges) with other cysteines, stabilizing protein structure.

General Amino Acid Structure

All amino acids share a common backbone structure consisting of:

Amino Group (-NH₂)

Located at the N-terminus of the protein. In physiological pH, it's protonated to -NH₃⁺. This group participates in peptide bond formation with the carboxyl group of another amino acid.

Carboxyl Group (-COOH)

Located at the C-terminus of the protein. In physiological pH, it's deprotonated to -COO⁻. This group is crucial for peptide bond formation and provides acidic properties.

Alpha Carbon (Cα)

The central carbon atom bonded to the amino group, carboxyl group, a hydrogen atom, and the distinctive R group (side chain). This is where all amino acids differ from each other.

R Group (Side Chain)

The unique side chain that distinguishes each amino acid. R groups vary in size, polarity, and charge. These differences determine how amino acids interact with each other and their environment.

Peptide Bond Formation

Amino acids link together through peptide bonds in a condensation reaction. The carboxyl group of one amino acid reacts with the amino group of the next, releasing a water molecule (H₂O). This forms a covalent C-N bond and creates the protein backbone.

🔗 Condensation Reaction

Also called dehydration synthesis. The hydroxyl (-OH) from the carboxyl group and a hydrogen (-H) from the amino group combine to form water. This reaction is catalyzed by ribosomes during protein synthesis.

⛓️ Polypeptide Chain

A chain of amino acids linked by peptide bonds. The chain has directionality: an N-terminus (free amino group) and a C-terminus (free carboxyl group). Proteins typically contain hundreds to thousands of amino acids.

🔬 Peptide Bond Characteristics

The C-N bond has partial double bond character (planarity). This restricts rotation around the peptide bond and affects protein structure. The bond is energetically stable and requires enzymatic hydrolysis to break.

🌍 Hydrolysis

The reverse of condensation. Peptide bonds are broken by hydrolysis, requiring water molecules. This occurs during digestion when proteases break down dietary proteins, and plays are critical for protein degradation in cells.
